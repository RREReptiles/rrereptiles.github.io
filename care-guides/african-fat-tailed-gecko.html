African Fat-Tailed Gecko Care Guide

Hemitheconyx caudicinctus

Terrestrial Nocturnal Moderate humidity

Provide generous floor space, a warm and cool hide, and a moist retreat. This is a more moisture-dependent species than a leopard gecko; a dry tank with only occasional spraying is inadequate.

Species Profile

Common name: African Fat-Tailed Gecko

Scientific name: Hemitheconyx caudicinctus

Adult size: 7-10 in (18-25 cm) total length

Captive lifespan: 15-20 years; some live longer. Typical captive lifespan is a planning estimate, not a measured statistical average.

Native range: West African savannas and forest margins

Habitat and activity: Terrestrial; nocturnal/crepuscular; sheltered burrows and humid cover

Diet: Varied cultured insects

Care difficulty: Beginner to Intermediate

Enclosure and Equipment

Shopping List

Secure enclosure at least 36 × 18 × 18 in (91 × 46 × 46 cm; L × W × H) for one adult; see Housing for exceptions and group arrangements.

Digital thermometers for different zones; a suitable hygrometer; timer for a consistent day/night cycle.

Housing

Our recommended starting adult enclosure is 36 × 18 × 18 in (91 × 46 × 46 cm; L × W × H) for one animal. Larger usable space is encouraged. These dimensions are a husbandry recommendation, not a species-specific experimentally proven threshold.

Provide multiple hides across the warm-to-cool and moist-to-drier gradient, covered travel routes and species-appropriate burrowing or low climbing structure. Fix heavy decor to the floor before adding loose substrate so digging cannot undermine it.

Juveniles may use a furnished adult enclosure when food intake, hydration and waste can be monitored. A temporary grow-out enclosure should still offer choice and cover. Quarantine newcomers separately with dedicated tools and a substrate that permits health monitoring; arrange veterinary assessment for unknown-origin animals.

Lighting and Temperature

Lighting & UVB

Provide roughly 10-12 hours of visible daylight followed by darkness. A measured UV Index (UVI) around 0.5-1.0 at the most exposed usable site, declining to near zero inside retreats, is our conservative starting recommendation. Low-level UVB is recommended when a usable gradient can be provided; nocturnal activity alone is not a reason to exclude light.

Measure exposure at the animal’s back height with an appropriate UVI meter, such as a Solarmeter 6.5. Lamp percentage, wattage and a fixed distance alone do not establish exposure: reflectors, mesh, perch height, plant growth and lamp age change output. Keep glass/plastic out of the UV path, follow fixture safety instructions and provide complete shade. Replace or reassess lamps according to measured output and manufacturer guidance.

Heating & Temperature

Localized basking surface: 90-95°F (32-35°C).

Cool-zone air: 73-78°F (23-26°C).

Nighttime air: 68-75°F (20-24°C) for healthy established animals.

Create the gradient on one side rather than heating every hide to the same temperature. Use a compatible thermostat on each heat source; a manual lamp dimmer does not replace thermostatic protection. Secure the probe and verify temperatures independently: probe thermometers measure air/retreat temperature, while an infrared thermometer measures exposed surfaces.

Use appropriately guarded overhead warmth when needed. Keep animals clear of hot fixtures, avoid heat rocks, and switch visible lights off at night. Night heat is needed only when the room falls below the appropriate range; use a thermostat-controlled lightless source if necessary.

Humidity and Hydration

Humidity & Ventilation

45-65% ambient; a consistently damp humid hide. Treat readings as a starting pattern rather than a number to chase regardless of substrate, airflow or animal behavior.

Measure humidity in a representative occupied zone, away from a freshly sprayed surface. Provide sheltered moisture choices while retaining airflow. Persistent condensation, sour odor, flooded soil or surfaces that never dry indicate a setup problem. Ventilation and hydration must be adjusted together rather than simply sealing all vents.

Water & Hydration

Provide a clean, appropriately shallow dish or accessible elevated water cup at all times. Change it daily and immediately when soiled; wash it regularly with dedicated tools. Potable water is generally suitable, with treatment where local water quality requires it.

For animals that drink droplets, mist foliage or suitable surfaces at the time they become active, then allow partial drying. Droplets supplement the dish; water chemistry claims should be based on evidence rather than a species’ trade name.

Substrate and Enrichment

Substrate

For quarantine, juveniles, or animals being monitored, paper towel is the safest and easiest substrate. For established adults, a natural substrate can be used.

A good DIY mix is 40% organic topsoil, 40% play sand, and 20% coco fiber, with a small patch of sphagnum moss in the humid hide. The substrate should hold light moisture but should not be dusty or soggy.

Feces and urates should be removed daily, and contaminated substrate should be scooped out and replaced. In non-bioactive enclosures, replace the substrate every 1-3 months depending on cleanliness.

Decor & Enrichment

Decorations help African fat-tailed geckos feel secure. Good decor can include flat hides, cork bark, fake foliage, leaf litter, small branches, and a humid hide filled with damp sphagnum moss or coco fiber.

Provide at least three hides: one warm, one cool, and one humid. More hides and cover usually result in a calmer, more reliable feeder.

Diet and Supplementation

Food & Feeding

African fat-tailed geckos are strict insectivores, which means that they eat insects and should not be fed fruit, vegetables, baby food, or crested gecko diet.

Good feeders include crickets, dubia roaches, discoid roaches, black soldier fly larvae, silkworms, mealworms, and small hornworms. Waxworms should be used only as occasional treats.

How often African fat-tailed geckos need to eat depends on age:

Juveniles - appropriately sized insects daily or every other day

Adults - insects 2-3x/week

Feed in the evening or near lights-out. Prey should be no wider than the space between the gecko's eyes. Remove uneaten crickets after feeding.

Supplements

Lightly dust appropriate insect meals with calcium and use a balanced reptile multivitamin containing suitable vitamin A according to the product’s directions. Gut-loading and variety support nutrition but do not eliminate the need to balance calcium-poor prey.

Match D3 supplementation to the actual UVB exposure and to vitamins already included in other products. UVB does not automatically mean zero dietary D3, and lack of UVB does not justify unlimited D3. Follow one coherent program; do not combine multiple strong vitamin products or coat every feeder heavily.

Handling and Cohabitation

Handling & Behavior

Let the animal settle and feed consistently before brief optional handling. Support the body, work low over a safe surface and stop when repeated retreat, escape attempts or defensive behavior show stress. Never lift a lizard by its tail.

Avoid handling immediately after meals, during difficult sheds or when unwell. Wash hands and use dedicated equipment to reduce disease transfer.

Cohabitation

Individual housing is the safest routine default. Shared housing can cause food competition, injury or reproductive pressure even without obvious fighting. Never assume a male/female pair or female group is compatible. Breeding introductions belong in a planned specialist program with separate backup enclosures and individual monitoring.

Routine and Seasonal Care

Cleaning & Routine Maintenance

Daily: check the animal’s access to water/appropriate moisture, temperatures, visible condition and equipment. Remove feces, contaminated material, dead feeders and spoiled food promptly.

Regularly: clean dishes and feeding surfaces, check thermostats and probes, inspect decor stability and maintain ventilation. Bioactive systems still require waste checks, food removal, plant care and drainage maintenance; they are not self-cleaning.

Record appetite, activity, shed/molt history and weight or body condition when safe. Arrange veterinary assessment for unexplained changes. Quarantine new animals with dedicated tools, and disinfect only with a suitable product used at the label dilution/contact time, then rinse and dry as required before return.

Shedding, Molting & Seasonal Changes

Maintain access to a clean humid retreat and inspect toes, tail tip and eyes after shedding when observation is possible. Do not peel retained skin or apply oils/adhesives. Persistent constriction, damaged tissue or repeated failed sheds warrants veterinary help.

Seasonal changes in appetite or activity can occur. Check temperatures, hydration and weight before treating prolonged refusal as normal. Deliberate brumation or breeding cycles require healthy animals and species-specific preparation; pet care does not require forcing a seasonal shutdown.

Common Health Concerns

Poor body condition or feeding decline: check the full gradient, hydration and food quality; arrange assessment for persistent refusal accompanied by weight loss.

Calcium/vitamin imbalance: weakness, tremors, a soft or misshapen jaw or abnormal limbs require veterinary assessment; extra high-dose supplements are not a substitute for diagnosis.

Skin, shed or injury problems: repeated retained skin, wounds, swelling or abnormal discoloration deserve prompt attention.

Respiratory or systemic illness: labored/open-mouth breathing at rest, marked lethargy, loss of balance, persistent diarrhea or rapid deterioration require an experienced exotic veterinarian.
