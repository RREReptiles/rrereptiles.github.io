Regal Jumping Spider Care Guide

Phidippus regius

Arboreal Diurnal visual hunter Easy to Intermediate

Regal jumping spiders are active visual predators from the southeastern United States and Caribbean. Good care is less about creating a permanently humid terrarium and more about combining excellent ventilation, reliable drinking droplets, bright daytime light, safe live prey, and an undisturbed upper silk retreat.

Care-standard note: jumping-spider husbandry is heavily debated online. This guide is written specifically for P. regius and separates well-supported natural history and broad experienced-keeper consensus from claims that have not been demonstrated, such as a routine UVB requirement.

Enclosure and Ventilation

  • A well-furnished 4 x 4 x 8 inch enclosure is a practical adult minimum; larger females can use 5 x 5 x 8 to 6 x 6 x 9 inches or similar.
  • Use fine cross-ventilation on opposing sides and an escape-proof front-, side-, or bottom-opening design.
  • Provide upper cork, branches, foliage, and sheltered anchor points without repeatedly destroying the spider's hammock.

Lighting and Temperature

  • Provide about 12 hours of bright, diffuse visible light followed by real darkness; a low-heat white LED works well.
  • UV perception exists in jumping spiders, but a captive UVB requirement has not been demonstrated for P. regius, so UVB is not part of our standard recommendation.
  • Maintain roughly 72-80°F during the day with a modest nighttime drop. Avoid direct sun, hot basking lamps, and prolonged temperatures above roughly 82-85°F in tiny enclosures.

Hydration and Humidity

  • Aim for moderate humidity, roughly 50-70%, with strong airflow rather than constant condensation or wet substrate.
  • Lightly mist one wall or a few leaves every 1-3 days as a starting point so small drinking droplets are available, then allow most surfaces to dry.
  • Do not spray the spider directly, flood the enclosure, or rely on open water dishes for spiderlings and small juveniles.

Feeding and Molting

  • Use varied cultured prey sized conservatively: fruit flies for small juveniles; flies, small roaches, and carefully sized crickets for larger spiders.
  • Feed growing juveniles more often and adults roughly every 4-7 days as a starting point, then adjust to abdomen size, activity, and life stage.
  • When a spider seals itself into a thick molt hammock, remove dangerous prey, keep hydration available away from the nest, and do not disturb the retreat until the spider emerges and hardens.

Handling, Egg Sacs and Safety

  • House one spider per enclosure. Handling is optional; let the spider step onto a clean hand or catch cup and keep interactions low over a safe surface.
  • Mature females can lay infertile sacs, and previously mated females can store sperm and produce multiple fertile sacs. An unknown-history female should be treated as potentially fertile.
  • Keep the enclosure away from insecticides, aerosols, flea/tick products, fragrances, essential oils, and recently treated pest-control areas.

Red Rocks Exotic Reptiles care information is educational support only. If a spider shows severe injury, persistent neurologic-looking movement, repeated failed molts, or unexplained rapid decline, seek help from an experienced arachnid keeper or exotic veterinarian familiar with invertebrates.
